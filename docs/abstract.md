This is the midterm project from my graduate course in data visualization at UMBC. For this project, I explored seasonal trends in relationships across age groups. The concept of "cuffing season" is the idea that people seek romantic relationships during colder months, but it is debated whether this is due to biological factors, pressure to have a partner during the holidays, or a result of young people meeting during a new school year. Cuffing season frequently refers to the October-February months, though its focus is on relationships that begin before the holidays. Personal experience and anecdotal evidence led me to the decision to explore this idea further and ask whether cuffing season is observable in relationship data, whether the phenomenon differs across age groups, and if relationships that begin during "cuffing season" are more likely to fail, like the discussion of the trend suggests.

The data for this project comes from a 2017 survey by Stanford University called How Couples Meet and Stay Together (HCMST). With this dataset I examined reported meeting dates, relationship start dates, breakup dates, and respondent ages. My three approaches to exploring the data were to compare the seasonal patterns across age groups of meeting partners, investigating how long couples take to transition from meeting to starting a relationship by time of year, and comparing the outcomes of relationships across seasons. Survey weights were incorporated in these explorations to account for sampling bias.

The original idea for this project was to simply explore through data visualization whether cuffing season exists but as I learned more about the concept I wanted to examine its relationship to age groups and relationship timing to answer the questions I felt existing research left unanswered. I was inspired by an in-class reading about crosswords to create faceted waffle plots, one for each age group, to compare the seasonal trends, but I decided on using a mosaic chart as I felt it fit the data better and allowed readers to see the trend across age groups more directly. I wanted to be sure that I didn't simply show 3 unrelated charts and try to create a story from them but rather create one chart that tackled my original question, and branch the following visualizations off of the questions that remained from looking at the first mosaic.
